% derive the explicit and implicit formulae for y(t) by Taylor explanding y(t) around t_0 and t_1
The Taylor expansion of a function is :
$$\sum^n \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n$$
Expanding around $y(t_0) = y_0$ and $y(t_1) = y_1$, we get:
$$y(t) = y_0 + \frac{dy_0}{dt}(t-t_0)$$
And:
$$y(t) = y_1 + \frac{dy_1}{dt}(t-t_1)$$

So, if we rewrite these equations, we obtain:
\begin{equation*}
    \centering
    \begin{split}
        y(t) &= y_0 + \frac{dy_0}{dt}(t-t_0)\\
        \Leftrightarrow y(t) - y_0 &= f(y_0, t_0) (t-t_0)\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{y_1 - y_0}{\Delta t} &= f(y_0, t_0)
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}

Similarly:
\begin{equation*}
    \centering
    \begin{split}
        y(t) &= y_1 + \frac{dy_1}{dt}(t-t_1)\\
        \Leftrightarrow y(t) - y_1 &= f(y_1, t_1) (t-t_1)\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{y_1 - y_0}{\Delta t} &= f(y_1, t_1)
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Exercise 4}
To implement a centred derivative, we could use a different method to find a new point away from the initial value, then use this new point to implement the method, then create another one further away with the first method. The projected one does not have to be extremely accurate, as it will be recalculated in the next step. However, if it is too inacurate, the error will only keep growing, as following points are based in part on this one.

Another way of doing this would be to project a new point extremely far with another method, then find a point in the middle, then keep using the centred method on these new points until sufficient precision is reached. The problem here would be the accuracy of the first point, as the step would have to be extremely big, and thus not extremely accurate. As the accuracy of the other points depend in part on it, it might cause some larger accuracy further down the line.

\subsection{Exercise 6}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \frac{dn_{^2He}}{dt} &= r_1n_p^2 - r_2n_{^2He}n_p\\
        \frac{dn_{^3He}}{dt} &= r_2n_{^2He}n_p - r_3n_{^3He}^2\\
        \frac{dn_{^4He}}{dt} &= r_3n_{^3He}^2\\
        \frac{dn_p}{dt} &= -r_1n_p^2 - r_2 n_pn_{^2He} + r_3n_{^3He}^2
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Where $r_1$, the reaction rate for reaction 17 is: $10^{-43}$ $cm^3/s$, $r_2$, the reaction rate for relation 18 is: $10^{-26}$ $cm^3/s$ and $r_3$, the reaction rate for relation 19 is: $10^{-34}$ $cm^3/s$.

To find the jacobian, we need to determine each of its elements:
$$J_{ij} = \frac{\partial \tilde{y_i}}{\partial y_j}$$

Where $\tilde{y_i}$ is the function $\frac{y_{i,1} - y_{i,0}}{dt} - f(y_{i,1}, t_{i,1})$. In this case, y is the number density of one of the elements. Let us start with taking $y \rightarrow n_{^2He}$:

\begin{equation*}
\centering
\begin{split}
\tilde{n}_{^2He} &= \frac{n_{^2He, 1} - n_{^2He, 0}}{dt} - r_1n_p^2 + r_2n_{^2He, 1}n_p\\
\Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^2He}}{\partial n_{^3He}} &= 0\\
\Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^2He}}{\partial n_{^4He}} &= 0\\
\Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^2He}}{\partial n_{p}} &= -2r_1n_p + r_2n_{^2He, 1}\\
\Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^2He}}{\partial n_{^2He}} &= \frac{1 - n_{^2He, 0}}{dt} + r_2n_p\\
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

Then for $n_{^3He}$:
\begin{equation*}
    \centering
    \begin{split}
        \tilde{n}_{^3He} &= \frac{n_{^3He, 1} - n_{^3He, 0}}{dt} - r_2n_{^2He}n_p + r_3n^2_{^3He, 1}\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^3He}}{\partial n_{^2He}} &= -r_2n_p\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^3He}}{\partial n_{^4He}} &= 0\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^3He}}{\partial n_p} &= - r_2n_{^2He}\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^3He}}{\partial n_{^3He}} &= \frac{1 - n_{^3He, 0}}{dt} + 2r_3n_{^3He, 1}\\
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}

And $n_{^4He}$
\begin{equation*}
    \centering
    \begin{split}
        \tilde{n}_{^4He} &= \frac{n_{^4He, 1} - n_{^4He, 0}}{dt} - r_3n_{^3He}^2\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^4He}}{\partial n_{^2He}} &= 0\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^4He}}{\partial n_{^3He}} &= -2r_3n_{^3He}\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^4He}}{\partial n_p} &= 0\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{^4He}}{\partial n_{^4He}} &= \frac{1 - n_{^4He, 0}}{dt}\\
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}

Finally for $n_p$:
\begin{equation*}
    \centering
    \begin{split}
        \tilde{n}_{p} &= \frac{n_{p, 1} - n_{p, 0}}{dt} + r_1n_p^2 + r_2 n_pn_{^2He} - r_3n_{^3He}^2\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{p}}{\partial n_{^2He}} &= r_2n_p\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{p}}{\partial n_{^3He}} &= - 2r_3n_{^3He}\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{p}}{\partial n_{^4He}} &= 0\\
        \Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial \tilde{n}_{p}}{\partial n_{p}} &= \frac{1 - n_{p, 0}}{dt} + 2r_1n_p + r_2n_{^2He}\\
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}

So the complete jacobian is :
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
    \frac{1 - n_{p, 0}}{dt} + 2r_1n_p + r_2n_{^2He} &  r_2n_p & - 2r_3n_{^3He} &  0\\
    -2r_1n_p + r_2n_{^2He, 1} & \frac{1 - n_{^2He, 0}}{dt} + r_2n_p & 0 & 0\\
    - r_2n_{^2He} & -r_2n_p & \frac{1 - n_{^3He, 0}}{dt} + 2r_3n_{^3He, 1} & 0\\
    0 & 0 & -2r_3n_{^3He} & \frac{1 - n_{^4He, 0}}{dt}\\
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

And we are excluding the 1/dt terms, so we find:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
    2r_1n_p + r_2n_{^2He} &  r_2n_p & - 2r_3n_{^3He} &  0\\
    -2r_1n_p + r_2n_{^2He} & r_2n_p & 0 & 0\\
    - r_2n_{^2He} & -r_2n_p & 2r_3n_{^3He} & 0\\
    0 & 0 & -2r_3n_{^3He} & 0\\
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

To find the stiffness of the system, we need to calculate its eigenvalues. The code used to do so can be found in \emph{Code/Exercises/SysStiffness.py}. There seem to be an error in the calculation of the Jacobian, as the eigenvalues seem strange. However, by playing around with the densities in this system, and with a fixed number densities for the protons, we can make some observations. The stiffness varies most with the density of deuterium, and least with $^4He$. It is stiffer when these densities are lower, and less stiff when they are higher. If the densities are too low, the eigenvalues are complex and it is more complicated to calculate the stiffness.

\subsection{Exercise 7}
a. There are 4 types of particles, and so 4 equations are needed to describe the system.
\linebreak

\noindent b. The system of equations is :
\begin{equation*}
\centering
\begin{split}
    \frac{dn_{Cr}}{dt} &= -r_1n_{Cr}n_\alpha\\
    \frac{dn_{Fe}}{dt} &= r_1n_{Cr}n_\alpha-r_2n_{Fe}n_\alpha\\
    \frac{dn_{Ni}}{dt} &= r_2n_{Fe}n_\alpha\\
    \frac{dn_\alpha}{dt} &= -r_1n_{Cr}n_\alpha-r_2n_{Fe}n_\alpha
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
So the Jacobian is :
\begin{equation*}
    \centering
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -r_1n_{Cr} - r_2n_{Fe} & -r_1n_\alpha & -r_2n_{Fe} & 0\\
        -r_1n_{Cr} & -r_1n_\alpha & 0 & 0\\
        r_1n_{Cr} - r_2n_{Fe} & r_1n_\alpha & -r_2n_\alpha & 0\\
        r_2n_{Fe} & 0 & r_2n_\alpha & 0
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation*}


\noindent c. If we add the radioactive decay of $^{56}Ni$ to the system, then we need to add terms to most of the equations, and thus the terms of the Jacobian. The $^{56}Ni$ might decay to $^{52}Fe$, also releasing $\alpha$ particles in the process. Then there needs to be new destructive terms in the $^{56}Ni$ terms, and new constructive ones in the $^{52}Fe$ and $\alpha$ ones. Depending on what the $^{56}Ni$ decays to, there might also be a new equation in the system, representing a new particle. This particle would only have constructive terms.

Similarly, photodissociation would add new destructive terms to the equations already in the system, and possibly constructive terms to new equations. This would depend on what the particles already present decay to.

Finally, three-body reactions would also add both constructive and destructive terms to the already present equations, and create new ones, thus also adding terms to the Jacobian, and new rows and columns. For example, the three-body reaction $3\alpha \rightarrow ^{12}C$ would add a destructive term to the alpha equation, and thus also in the first term of the Jacobian (term $J_{11}$). It would also create a new equation, and a new row and collumn with a constructive term to add the presence of $^{12}C$ to the system.
